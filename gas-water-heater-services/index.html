Most gas water heaters in Salisbury, MD work the same basic way. Gas enters through a control valve that doubles as the thermostat and feeds a burner beneath the tank, and the hot exhaust travels up a flue through the middle of the tank and out through the vent. A thermocouple, or a flame sensor on newer electronic models, tells the valve a flame is present; without that signal, the valve closes for safety. The design is dependable, but it has a few predictable weak spots. Thermocouples wear out, pilot tubes and burner ports clog, the flame arrestor screen at the base packs up with lint and dust, control valves fail, and vents lose their draft.

Salisbury Water Heater does not guess with gas appliances. On every Salisbury call, our technicians test the thermocouple's millivolt output, inspect the pilot and burner flame, check gas pressure at the valve, and run a draft test at the draft hood to confirm exhaust is actually leaving the house. We look inside the combustion chamber for scorching, rust, and signs of flame rollout, which tell us whether the flue is blocked or the burner is misbehaving, and we check for carbon monoxide around the heater. One rule comes before all of it: if you smell gas, do not light anything or flip switches. Leave the house, call your gas utility from outside, and then call us once it is safe.

Warning Signs to Watch For

Catching a symptom early is often the difference between a quick part swap and a tank that gives out on the garage floor. Keep an eye out for the following:

  • A tank that pops and bangs while it heats, a classic sign of sediment
  • Less hot water than the tank should deliver
  • Leaks at the drain valve, the fittings, or the bottom of the tank
  • A pilot light that will not stay lit
  • Signs of backdrafting, such as soot, condensation, or an exhaust smell at the vent
  • A yellow, lazy, or sooty burner flame instead of a crisp blue one
  • A rotten-egg odor near the water heater, in which case get out and call the gas company first
  • A cold tank and a burner that will not come on

What a Trained Technician Brings to the Job

Two things make gas water heater work a poor DIY project: the parts involve fuel and combustion, and the symptoms overlap. A pilot outage might come from a worn thermocouple, a partly blocked pilot tube, lint on the flame arrestor screen, a downdraft, or an internal valve failure. A wrong guess there just wastes money. Guess wrong on a gas fitting or vent connection and the stakes are much higher, since a small leak or a spillage problem can put gas or carbon monoxide into the living space. Gas valve and heater replacements also typically call for a permit.

Our technicians approach gas heaters with instruments rather than assumptions. They measure thermocouple millivolts, read gas pressure at the valve, test draft at the hood, check for CO around the appliance, and leak-test every joint they touch. That process fixes the actual fault the first time and leaves your home safer than before. We also give you an honest assessment of the heater itself. A tank past about ten or twelve years with a failing valve or a leak is usually a replacement, and you will hear that from us before you spend money on a repair.

How to Prevent Water Heater Problems

A few preventive steps can help you avoid a cold shower or a wet floor down the road. Here are our recommendations:

  • Vacuum lint and dust from the flame arrestor screen at the base a few times a year
  • Give the heater breathing room and keep flammables well away from it
  • Keep working CO alarms near bedrooms and on each floor
  • Check the anode rod every three to five years, since it protects the tank from corrosion
  • Choose a setting of about 120 degrees to balance comfort, safety, and efficiency
  • Drain sediment from the bottom of the tank once or twice a year
  • Make sure the vent pipe stays connected, sloped upward, and free of rust
